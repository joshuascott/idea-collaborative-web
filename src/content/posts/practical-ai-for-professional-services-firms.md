A 14-person accounting and advisory firm in Loveland had the same problem every firm its size has. The billable work was fine. It was everything wrapped around the billable work that ran into the evening: the follow-up email after the client call, the proposal that took a Saturday, the stack of client documents somebody had to key in before the real work could start.

None of that is exotic. All three are text going in and text coming out, which is exactly where these tools are strongest and where the setup is smallest. Here's what each one looks like and how to check whether it pays.

## 1. From the call to the follow-up

The task: a 50-minute client meeting becomes a summary, a decision list, and an email with who owes what by when. Done properly it takes 30 to 40 minutes. Done at 6pm it takes 15 and it's thin.

The tool category is a meeting recorder with transcription and summarization, or a general assistant fed a transcript you already have. Either way, the important part is not the summary. It's the second prompt: turn this into a client-ready email with decisions, open items, owners, and dates, and flag anything I said I would send.

Realistic result is 8 to 10 minutes including your read-through. Get consent to record, every time, and say plainly whose tool it is.

## 2. Proposal and engagement letter first drafts

The task: writing an engagement scope from a discovery conversation. At most firms this is 3 to 5 hours, often on a weekend, and it's the reason proposals go out on Wednesday instead of Monday.

The material that makes this work is already in your drive. Take your 5 best past engagement letters, hand them over with the notes from the new conversation, and ask for a first draft in the same structure. You get a draft in about 10 minutes and then you do the part that's actually yours: the scope boundaries, the fee, the assumptions, the things you refuse to promise.

Call it an hour instead of 4. The gain isn't only the 3 hours. It's the proposal going out 2 days sooner.

## 3. Document intake

The task: turning what the client sent into something structured. Fourteen PDFs into a checklist. A folder of receipts into categories. Prior-year statements into the fields your workpaper needs.

This is the one with the highest error cost, so it needs the tightest review. The rule that makes it safe: the output must be checkable against the source in under 5 minutes, and it must show where each value came from. If somebody has to open all 14 documents again to verify it, you've moved the work rather than reduced it.

## The arithmetic

Take your blended hourly rate. If it's $175 and one of these gives a single person 4 hours a week back, that's roughly 16 hours a month of capacity. Whether that becomes revenue or evenings is your decision, and it's worth making on purpose rather than letting it fill itself.

The tools for all 3 workflows cost less per month than one billable hour. That's why this math almost always works and why it isn't the interesting question. The interesting question is whether anyone actually changes how they work, which is why you start with one workflow and one person rather than three and everyone.

## Where to start

Pick the workflow where the input already exists in writing. If you record meetings, start with follow-ups. If you have 20 past proposals in a folder, start with proposals. The one with the material already sitting there will be working in 2 weeks. The other two will still be there when you're ready.
